# The Three Financial Statements

Accounting is the language of business. Every public company publishes three core financial statements, and nearly every number used in valuation (revenue, profit, cash flow, debt) comes from them. You don't need to be an accountant, but you do need to read them fluently and know how they connect.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> - The **income statement** shows performance *over a period*: did the company make a profit?
> - The **balance sheet** shows the financial position *at a moment*: what does it own and owe?
> - The **cash flow statement** shows where the cash *actually* came from and went.
>
> They're linked. Profit flows into the balance sheet through retained earnings, and the cash flow statement explains the change in the cash balance.

## The balance sheet

The balance sheet always balances because of the **accounting identity**:

\[ \text{Assets} = \text{Liabilities} + \text{Shareholders' Equity} \]

Everything the firm owns (assets) was financed either by borrowing (liabilities) or by the owners (equity). Equity is the residual claim: what would be left for owners if all assets were sold at book value and all debts repaid.

| Assets | Liabilities & Equity |
|---|---|
| **Current assets** (turn into cash within a year): cash, accounts receivable, inventory | **Current liabilities** (due within a year): accounts payable, short-term debt |
| **Long-term assets:** property, plant & equipment (PP&E), intangibles | **Long-term liabilities:** bonds, long-term loans |
| | **Equity:** paid-in capital + retained earnings |

## The income statement

```
  Revenue (sales)
− Cost of goods sold (COGS)
= Gross profit
− Operating expenses (salaries, rent, marketing, depreciation…)
= Operating income  (EBIT: earnings before interest and taxes)
− Interest expense
= Earnings before taxes (EBT)
− Taxes
= Net income
```

**Depreciation** spreads the cost of a long-lived asset (an oven, a truck) over its useful life. It reduces profit each year, but **no cash leaves the firm** when depreciation is recorded. The cash left when the asset was bought. Keep that in mind; it's why profit and cash flow differ.

## The cash flow statement

| Section | What it captures |
|---|---|
| **Operating activities (CFO)** | Cash generated by running the business |
| **Investing activities (CFI)** | Buying or selling long-term assets (capital expenditures, acquisitions) |
| **Financing activities (CFF)** | Borrowing, repaying debt, issuing shares, paying dividends, buybacks |

Most companies use the **indirect method** for operating cash flow. It starts from net income and undoes the non-cash items:

\[ CFO = \text{Net income} + \text{Depreciation} - \Delta\text{Receivables} - \Delta\text{Inventory} + \Delta\text{Payables} \]

Why subtract an increase in receivables? Because you recorded the sale as revenue but haven't collected the cash yet. Why add an increase in payables? Because you recorded the expense but haven't paid the supplier yet.

## Building all three statements in Python

Let's follow a small coffee-roasting company, **Bean Co.**, through one year. We start with last year's balance sheet, record this year's activity, and check that everything ties out. (All figures in $ thousands.)

```python run
# Balance sheet at the END of last year (= start of this year)
opening = {
    "cash": 50, "receivables": 30, "inventory": 40, "ppe": 200,
    "payables": 25, "debt": 120, "common_stock": 100, "retained_earnings": 75,
}

def check_balance(bs):
    assets = bs["cash"] + bs["receivables"] + bs["inventory"] + bs["ppe"]
    claims = bs["payables"] + bs["debt"] + bs["common_stock"] + bs["retained_earnings"]
    return assets, claims

print("Opening: assets = %d, liabilities + equity = %d" % check_balance(opening))
```

Now this year's income statement, using the `IncomeStatement` class from the library:

```python run
from finance101.statements import IncomeStatement

revenue, cogs, sga, depreciation, interest = 500, 300, 100, 20, 8
tax_rate = 0.25
ebit = revenue - cogs - sga - depreciation
taxes = (ebit - interest) * tax_rate

inc = IncomeStatement(revenue=revenue, cost_of_goods_sold=cogs,
                      operating_expenses=sga + depreciation,
                      interest_expense=interest, tax_expense=taxes)

print(f"Revenue          {inc.revenue:>6.0f}")
print(f"Gross profit     {inc.gross_profit:>6.0f}")
print(f"Operating income {inc.operating_income:>6.0f}   (EBIT)")
print(f"Net income       {inc.net_income:>6.0f}")
```

During the year, Bean Co. also:

- let receivables grow to 40 and inventory to 45 as sales grew,
- owed suppliers 30 at year end (payables),
- bought a new roaster for 50 (capital expenditure),
- repaid 10 of debt and paid 14 in dividends.

```python run
dividends, capex, debt_repaid = 14, 50, 10
closing_receivables, closing_inventory, closing_payables = 40, 45, 30

# --- Cash flow statement (indirect method) ---
cfo = (inc.net_income + depreciation
       - (closing_receivables - opening["receivables"])
       - (closing_inventory - opening["inventory"])
       + (closing_payables - opening["payables"]))
cfi = -capex
cff = -debt_repaid - dividends
net_change = cfo + cfi + cff

print(f"Cash from operations  {cfo:>6.0f}")
print(f"Cash from investing   {cfi:>6.0f}")
print(f"Cash from financing   {cff:>6.0f}")
print(f"Net change in cash    {net_change:>6.0f}")
```

Finally, the closing balance sheet. Every line follows from the other two statements:

```python run
closing = {
    "cash": opening["cash"] + net_change,                         # from the cash flow statement
    "receivables": closing_receivables,
    "inventory": closing_inventory,
    "ppe": opening["ppe"] + capex - depreciation,                 # buy new, depreciate old
    "payables": closing_payables,
    "debt": opening["debt"] - debt_repaid,
    "common_stock": opening["common_stock"],
    "retained_earnings": opening["retained_earnings"] + inc.net_income - dividends,  # the key link
}
for item in closing:
    print(f"{item:<18} {opening[item]:>6.0f} → {closing[item]:>6.0f}")
print("\nClosing: assets = %.0f, liabilities + equity = %.0f" % check_balance(closing))
```

It balances. That's a **three-statement model**, the foundation of every financial model built on Wall Street, in about 30 lines of Python.

> [!TIP]
> Change `capex` to 80 and re-run the last two cells. Cash falls, PP&E rises, and the balance sheet *still* balances. Now try changing only `closing_receivables`. If you edit just one statement by hand and forget the others, the model breaks. That's why analysts always build the "balance check".

## Profit is not cash

Bean Co. earned a net income of 54 but generated 64 of operating cash and spent 50 on a roaster. A fast-growing company can be very profitable on paper and still run out of cash, because growth ties up money in receivables, inventory and new equipment. Many bankruptcies happen to *profitable* firms.

**Free cash flow** measures the cash left for investors after the business has funded its operations and investments:

\[ FCF = CFO - \text{Capital expenditures} \]

```python run
fcf = cfo - capex
print(f"Free cash flow: {fcf:.0f}  (vs. net income of {inc.net_income:.0f})")
```

We'll use free cash flow to value projects in Chapter 9 and companies in Chapter 11.

## Where to find real statements

US public companies file an annual report (**10-K**) and quarterly reports (**10-Q**) with the SEC. They are free on EDGAR. The statements are in "Item 8: Financial Statements and Supplementary Data".

## Key takeaways

- **Balance sheet:** Assets = Liabilities + Equity, at a point in time.
- **Income statement:** revenue minus expenses equals net income, over a period.
- **Cash flow statement:** operating, investing and financing cash flows explain the change in cash.
- Net income links to the balance sheet through **retained earnings**; the cash flow statement links through **cash**.
- Profit ≠ cash. Depreciation and working-capital changes create the gap.
